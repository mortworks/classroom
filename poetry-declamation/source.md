# Poetry declamation sessions (Second Form Omnia)

:::teacher
**General notes on delivering the Poetry Declamation mini-course** 
- Note on video poems: the poems in the clips are *not* in the anthology pupils receive. They are models only.
- **Important:** set a “Choose and learn your poem by heart” assignment at the end of Session 1. Due by Session 3 (two weeks later). Pupils must choose from the anthology distributed in the lesson.
:::


## Session 1 — What makes a good reading? Choosing a poem

:::teacher
Aim:
- introduce the idea of meaningful, controlled delivery of poems via professional examples
- practise diction and pacing
- help pupils choose a poem.

Timing guide (40 mins):
- Intro 2 mins
- Example to critique: Sonnet 29 + clip 8
- Further examples: Contrasts 10
- Reading aloud microskills 7–8
- Choose poem 8–10
- First readings 5
:::

### Introduction

You are preparing for the poetry **declamation competition** as part of *Omnia Onstage*.

**Poetry declamation is the art of reading poetry aloud before an audience. It goes back to the Ancient Greeks.**

- You will choose a poem from the anthology (you will be given this).
- You will have two weeks to learn your chosen poem off by heart!
- You will perform it in the class heat in two weeks’ time.

### 1. Example of poetry declamation: Sonnet 29 + Judi Dench

:::teacher
a) Present the poem:
- Read it aloud yourself to the class (or, if you prefer, ask a confident volunteer pupil to do so)
- Explain the core movement in the poem: despair → reflection → love → transformation.

b) Set up the clip:
- Explain the nature of the Graham Norton Show (lively atmosphere).
- Dame Judi is an actress who has spent her life memorising and performing Shakespeare.
- Preview the things in the video pupils should look for (see list below)
:::

{{link:sonnet_29}}

{{video:dench_sonnet29}}

While watching, look for:

- Dame Judi's **pace**
- Where she **slows down**
- The clarity of her **consonants**
- **Posture** - the way she holds herself
- **Stillness and gesture**
- The **mood** in the studio / how the room responds

Afterwards:

- How did she use **pace**?
- Where did she **slow down**?
- How clear were her **consonants**?
- Did she move? If not, why not? What did you notice about her posture?
- How did the people around her respond? What was the mood in the studio like?

### 2. Contrasting delivery styles

:::teacher
Show one or two professional readings plus one or two Poetry By Heart finalists.

The intention here is to get pupils to appreciate that declamation can happen in different ways. Some may prefer a more animated and involved delivery — others, a quiet but clear intensity.
:::

Suggested clips:
- {{link:mirren_tyger}}
- {{link:branagh_sonnet30}}
- {{link:damian_lewis_words}}
- {{link:poetry_by_heart}}

Prompt questions:

- What do these readers have in common?
- Where do they differ in style?
- Did anything in the more animated readings feel **added-on**, or did it genuinely support the poem?
- Which style appeals to you?

### 3. Your turn! Microskills: diction + pacing

#### Diction practice

:::teacher
Model what “clean endings” sound like. Explicitly mention swallowed consonants, especially at the ends of words. Then get pupils to practise enunciating some of the words below (point out that they are from Sonnet 29!)

Suggestion: (1) chorus practice, then (2) pairs. Keep it brisk.
:::

Say these clearly (don’t swallow the endings!):

- state
- fate
- least
- despising
- self
- like
- then
- hope

#### Pacing practice (use three lines)

Read these lines aloud in different ways and decide what feels right:

- “Wishing me like to one more rich in hope,”
- “Desiring this man’s art and that man’s scope,”
- “With what I most enjoy contented least;”

As you practise, focus on:

- clear diction
- a natural ebb and flow of pace
- breathing in sensible places (not necessarily line breaks)

### 4. Choosing a poem

Browse the anthology and have a look for a poem that you might like to read.

Your Criteria:

- you understand it (at least broadly)
- you like the sound of it
- you can imagine speaking it aloud

In class: **no online searches for readings.**

:::teacher
Optional: lightly encourage pupils to identify the poem’s “hinge” (where it turns or changes direction).
:::

### 5. First readings

Read your chosen poem aloud to a partner.

:::teacher
- Keep it low-stakes.
- No critique yet: this is gentle exposure and practice.
:::

## Session 2 — Learning by heart + developing delivery

:::teacher
Aim: refresh Session 1 principles; teach chunking/memorisation; practise choices; rehearse in small groups.

Timing guide (40 mins):
- Recap 5
- Whitman on page first 5
- Clip 5
- Memorisation 15
- Trios 10
:::

### 1. Quick recap

- What did we see good readers do? (and not do?)
- What did we work on last time? (consonants, pacing)

### 2. Read the Whitman poem before the clip

:::teacher
Make sure pupils see the poem first so the clip “lands”. Give them silent reading time before discussion.
Briefly sketch the emotional terrain: self-doubt → questioning → affirmation.
:::

Read silently first:

{{link:whitman_o_me_o_life}}

### 3. Dead Poets Society clip

{{video:dead_poets_whitman}}

After watching, discuss:

- What do you think of Mr Keating's (the teacher's) delivery of the poem?
- Where is he when he delivers it? Is this important?
- What gesture does he use (if any)?
- Is it a passionate delivery? Why / why not?

### 4. Starting out with memorisation

#### a) Techniques

What techniques could help you learn your poem by heart?

:::teacher
If you can, demonstrate chunking on a short extract (or use a pupil’s poem with permission).
Stress sense-units over line-units where helpful - e.g. if a sentence spills over into the next line.
:::

#### b) Quiet memorisation

Begin learning your poem by heart.

Use whatever method works for you (quiet repetition, covering lines, whispering, testing yourself).

:::teacher
Give pupils freedom here; many will have their own preferred methods. Circulate and gently coach.
:::

### 5. Trios rehearsal

In groups of three:

1. Say which poem you’ve chosen.
2. Read it aloud (it does not have to be from memory yet).
3. Receive constructive feedback.

:::teacher
Mix groups up if possible. Keep feedback supportive and specific. Keep groups on task.
:::

## Session 3 — Class performance heat

:::teacher
Aim: fair, confidence-building performance experience; select two finalists.

Timing guide (40 mins):
- Framing + warm-up 5
- Performances 25
- Scoring + brief feedback 10 (or weave feedback into the running order)
:::

### 1. Reassurance + framing + warm-up

You are judged on:

- **clarity**
- **understanding**
- **choices**
- **impact**

Remember:

- 'Drama' can come in different forms.
- Stillness and movement/gesture are both choices.
- You are declaiming the poem, not acting out a role.

Warm-up: recite your poem (from memory as far as possible) to a partner.

### 2. Performances

Each of you will declaim your poem!

Be a respectful, attentive audience.

:::teacher
Keep pace brisk but calm. Consider having a visible running order.
:::

### 3. Scoring

Four categories, 5 points each:

- clarity of voice
- understanding of meaning
- quality of delivery choices
- overall impact

:::teacher
You may allow a class vote, but the teacher decides.
:::

:::teacher
### 4. Constructive feedback

You will receive at least one sentence of constructive feedback.

Announce the finalists.

Try to keep feedback specific (“clear consonants at line endings”, “good control of pace”, “you made the turn land”).

End warm.
:::
